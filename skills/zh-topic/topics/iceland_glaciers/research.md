# Research: glaciers in Iceland (冰島的冰川)

Facts used in the video and the book. Every number here has a source; don't add
numbers to the script that aren't in this file.

## Size and coverage
- Glaciers cover about **10%** of Iceland (about 11% in 2008, about 10% in 2019); total
  glacier area about **10,400 km²** in 2019. [1][2]
- Largest: **Vatnajökull (瓦特納冰川)**, about **7,700 km²** today (older figures 8,100–8,300);
  usually **400–600 m** thick, at most about **950 m**. [1][3]
- Next: Langjökull (~950 km²), Hofsjökull (~925 km²), Mýrdalsjökull (~600 km²). [1]
- Iceland's highest peak, Hvannadalshnúkur, rises from the Vatnajökull area (Öræfajökull). [3]
- Vatnajökull National Park was founded on **7 June 2008**. [3]

## How a glacier forms, and why the ice is blue
- Snow builds up year after year; the weight of new snow presses the old snow into
  dense ice and squeezes the air out. [4]
- Dense old ice absorbs red light and scatters blue, so freshly exposed old ice looks
  deep blue (crevasse walls, ice caves, calving fronts). [4]

## Fire under the ice
- Volcanoes lie under the ice: **Grímsvötn** (most active) and **Bárðarbunga** (largest)
  under Vatnajökull; **Öræfajökull** under its south edge. [3]
- **Katla** lies under **Mýrdalsjökull**; last confirmed eruption **1918**. [5]
- Eruptions or geothermal heat melt ice and release sudden floods, **jökulhlaup**
  (冰川洪水). A 2011 Katla flood destroyed a Ring Road bridge; ~200 people were
  evacuated. Grímsvötn floods carry ash to the coast and help form the black sands. [3][5]

## Shrinking
- Since the late 1800s Iceland's glaciers have lost more than **2,200 km²** of area
  (about 18%). [1][2]
- 1995–2019: about **9.6 billion tonnes** of ice lost per year; 2000–2019 area shrank
  about **40 km² per year**. [2]
- **Okjökull (奧克冰川)** lost its glacier status in **2014** (the ice stopped moving), the
  first Icelandic glacier to do so; a plaque, "A letter to the future", was placed in
  **August 2019**. It says all of Iceland's glaciers are expected to follow within
  about **200 years**. [6]
- Projection: Iceland's glaciers may lose about **half their volume by 2100**; the
  three largest ice caps should still exist then. [2]
